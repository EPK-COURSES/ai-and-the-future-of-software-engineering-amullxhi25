# Personal Reflection

## AI Statistics and Industry Reports — What the Data Made Me Think

-----

When I started this research, I expected to find a straightforward story: AI is growing, developers are happy about it, productivity is going up. The statistics told a more complicated and, honestly, more interesting story than that.

The number that surprised me most was the trust gap. In 2025, 84% of developers are using or planning to use AI tools — but 46% say they distrust the accuracy of AI output. At first this seems contradictory. Why would you keep using a tool you don’t trust? The more I thought about it, the more it made sense. You use it because it saves time on certain tasks. You distrust it because you’ve seen it fail enough times to know you have to check everything. That is not a ringing endorsement of AI — that is a cautious, pragmatic relationship with a tool that has real limitations.

What concerns me most about AI in software engineering is not that it will replace developers. The data doesn’t really support that. What concerns me is the subtle erosion of skill that could happen if developers lean on AI too heavily before they have developed strong fundamentals. If a junior developer uses Copilot to write code they don’t fully understand, they are not learning to think about the problem — they are learning to accept suggestions. The 30% suggestion acceptance rate is somewhat reassuring here: experienced developers are filtering AI output carefully. But that filtering skill itself has to be developed, and it requires already knowing enough to recognize when the AI is wrong.

The productivity numbers are genuinely impressive. A 56% improvement in task completion speed is not a small thing. But reading the research carefully, those gains come from specific, bounded tasks — writing functions, generating boilerplate, refactoring code. The same developers who are using AI to write code faster are overwhelmingly rejecting AI for deployment decisions and project planning. They know the difference between a task where speed matters most and a task where a mistake could bring down a production system. That distinction requires judgment AI cannot provide.

What opportunities do I see? Honestly, quite a few. The McKinsey estimate of $2.6–$4.4 trillion in annual economic value is remarkable, and software engineering is one of the four biggest areas of impact. That suggests the field will grow, not shrink. But the roles that grow will be different. The ability to evaluate AI output, catch errors, design systems that AI cannot design on its own, and communicate technical decisions to non-technical stakeholders — these are the skills that the statistics consistently point toward as remaining irreplaceable.

Did my opinion change after doing this research? Yes, in one specific way. I came in thinking that skepticism about AI was mostly coming from people who were afraid of change. The data changed that view. The most skeptical group in the surveys is experienced developers — the people with the most knowledge. Their skepticism is not fear; it is expertise. That reframing matters to me. Being cautious about AI output is not a failure to adapt — it is what good engineering judgment looks like.

How will I personally adapt? I think the answer comes from what the statistics keep showing: AI is a tool, not a replacement for thinking. My goal is to understand what I am doing well enough to evaluate whatever AI produces. That means continuing to build strong foundations in computer science, keeping up with how AI tools are evolving, and practicing the judgment required to know when to use AI assistance and when to rely on independent reasoning. The developers in these surveys who use AI most effectively are not the ones who trust it the most — they are the ones who understand it well enough to know exactly where it can and cannot be trusted.

That is the skill worth developing.
